02 — the pour

How a job actually runs

Concrete is a one-shot material. The sequence below is why a slab either lasts or starts cracking in the first summer.

Priced from photos

Photos of the site with a tape measure, plus access notes. Size, thickness, finish and how far the truck can get decide the price.

Set out & formed

Levels taken, formwork set to the fall, base compacted and mesh laid on chairs so it finishes in the middle of the slab, not on the ground.

Poured & finished

Concrete placed, screeded, floated and finished — broom, trowel or aggregate — while it is still workable.

Cured & cut

Curing compound on, control joints saw-cut within 24–48 hours so the slab cracks where it is meant to, not where it wants to.

03 — finishes

Four finishes, four different jobs

The finish is decided before the pour, not after — each one changes the mix, the timing and the price.

BroomStandard for driveways and paths. Grippy when wet, hides minor marks, cheapest of the four.
Exposed aggregateStone washed back to the surface. Harder wearing, non-slip, the most tolerant of a slab that has to sit in full sun.
Honed / trowelSmooth, tight finish for entertaining areas and under cover. Needs sealing and shows water marks.
Saw-cutNot a surface finish — the control joints that keep a slab from cracking where it chooses. Cut on time or not at all.
04 — pricing

What decides the number

  1. Size and thicknessSquare metres at the thickness the job needs — 100mm for paths, 125mm for a car, more where something heavier drives on it.
  2. AccessWhether a truck can back up to the formwork or the concrete has to be wheeled or pumped. This is usually the biggest single difference between two quotes.
  3. RemovalBreaking out and disposing of the old slab, plus any excavation or spoil removal before the base goes down.
  4. The finishBroom is the baseline. Exposed aggregate, honing and sealing add labour and timing to the job.
  5. Council requirementsA new crossover usually needs a permit and an inspection — Melbourne councils differ, and the cost sits outside the pour itself.

No site visit needed to start. Send photos of the area, roughly how big it is, what it is for and where a truck can get to. Most jobs are priced from that the same day.

What changes the price later: an old slab that is thicker than expected, a service pipe in the way, or ground that needs more excavation than the photos showed. That gets said before the pour, not after.

What is included: set out, formwork, mesh and chairs, placing, finishing, curing compound and saw-cutting. Spoil removal is quoted with the job.

06 — questions

Asked before most quotes

Can you quote from photos?
Usually yes. Take a wide shot of the area, one from each end, and one with a tape measure against the longest edge. Add anything that affects access — a narrow side gate, steps, a low carport, a tree in the way.
How long before we can drive on it?
Light foot traffic the next day, a car after about seven days, and a heavy vehicle after 28. It keeps gaining strength well past the point it looks finished, so the timeline matters more than the surface feels.
Why does the slab need to be cut?
Concrete shrinks as it cures and will crack somewhere. Saw-cut control joints give it a straight line to crack along, within 24–48 hours of the pour. Miss that window and the joints do very little.
Do I need a council permit for a new crossover?
For the section between the property line and the road, usually yes. Most Melbourne councils require a permit and an inspection of that part of the work. It is separate from the concrete itself and gets flagged before anything is ordered.
Can you remove the old slab?
Yes — breaking out, cutting clean edges and disposing of the spoil are all part of a replacement job. It is quoted separately because the thickness of old concrete varies a lot.
What strength concrete do you use?
25MPa for driveways and vehicle areas, 20MPa for paths and garden work, and higher where an engineer specifies it for structural footings.
